How to Start Learning Piano as a Beginner: 15 Quick Tips (Everything You Need to Know)

AB By Alice Baker · 18 min read
🎹

Have you always wanted to play the piano but don't know where to start? Whether you're a complete beginner or an adult hoping to learn piano by yourself without spending a fortune on private lessons, you might be wondering: Is piano easy to learn? Do you need to read sheet music? What kind of keyboard should you buy? And how long will it take to play your first song?

The good news is that with the right instrument, a structured learning plan, and regular practice, you can make steady progress.

In this guide, we'll share 15 practical tips on how to start learning piano, from choosing your first keyboard and understanding piano notes to developing proper finger technique and building a daily practice routine.

1. Get the Right Instrument First

Before you start learning piano, you need an instrument you can practice on regularly. You don't have to spend thousands of dollars on a professional piano, for most beginners, a budget of $400 to $600 is a good starting point.

Tip 1. Start with a Digital Keyboard

A digital piano is usually a more practical starting point than an acoustic piano. An acoustic piano produces sound through hammers striking physical strings. A digital piano uses electronic sound samples or modeling to reproduce piano sounds.

Both can help you learn, but digital pianos have several advantages for beginners:

  • More affordable: You can find entry level digital pianos at a much lower price than acoustic pianos.
  • Space saving: Most digital models are compact and easier to move.
  • Headphone support: You can practice quietly without disturbing your family or neighbors.
  • Extra features: Some models include recording, metronomes, different instrument sounds, and learning functions.

For most beginners, a digital piano with weighted keys is a sensible investment. You can always consider upgrading to an acoustic piano after you've developed a consistent practice habit and know that you want to continue learning.

Tip 2. Get 88 Fully Weighted Keys and a Sustain Pedal

You might want to go smaller to save money and go for inexpensive keyboards with 61 keys. However, a 61 key keyboard is too limited for learning traditional piano because it doesn't cover the full range of notes found on an acoustic piano.

If your budget allows, choose an 88 key digital piano with hammer action weighted keys and a sustain pedal. You don't need an expensive professional model to get started. When shopping for your first piano, look for three essential features:

  1. 88 keys: A standard full size piano has 88 keys, giving you the complete range of notes you'll need as you progress.
  2. Fully weighted keys: Proper weighted keys that feel like a real piano. This helps you develop finger strength and control.
  3. A sustain pedal: A sustain pedal allows notes to continue sounding after you release the keys, making your playing smoother and more expressive.

Recommended budget brands include Roland FP-10, Yamaha P-45, Casio Privia and CDP series, Nux NPK-10, and Kurzweil KA90. All solid entry level options. If you want extra sounds beyond piano, the Yamaha CK88 is a great value.

Set up Proper Hand and Finger Positioning

Once you have your instrument, the next step is learning how to position your hands and body correctly. Proper posture helps you use your arm and finger muscles efficiently, apply the right amount of force when pressing the keys, and avoid unnecessary tension or strain while playing.

Tip 3. Curve Your Fingers Naturally and Avoid Flat Fingers

One of the first things you should learn is how to position your fingers on the keyboard. Imagine you're gently holding a small egg in your palm. Your fingers should form a natural curve rather than lying completely flat.

Here are a few things to remember:

  • Keep your fingers naturally curved, with each fingertip ready to press a key.
  • Use the soft pads near your fingertips to play the keys.
  • Keep your thumb relaxed and use its side naturally when needed.
  • Avoid collapsing or bending your finger joints inward.
  • Don't press the keys with excessive force.

Why does finger positioning matter?

If your fingers collapse or become too flat, you may struggle to control individual notes. Your fingers can also become less efficient when moving from one key to another.

Long fingernails can sometimes interfere with your finger angle and cause unwanted clicking sounds against the keys. Keep your nails short enough to allow comfortable contact with the keyboard.

Start slowly and pay attention to how your hands feel. Relaxed, controlled movements are much more important than playing quickly.

Tip 4. Sit Right, with Feet Flat, Knees Under the Keyboard, Relaxed Shoulders and Wrists

Good posture helps you play comfortably and avoid unnecessary tension. Before practicing, check your sitting position:

  1. Feet: Keep both feet flat on the floor. Your feet should feel stable rather than dangling.
  2. Knees: Position your knees comfortably under the keyboard, with enough space to move your legs.
  3. Back: Sit upright without leaning forward or hunching your shoulders.
  4. Shoulders: Keep your shoulders relaxed and avoid lifting them while playing.
  5. Wrists: Keep your wrists relaxed and generally level with your hands rather than letting them droop.

Your elbows should be around keyboard height or slightly above it, depending on your seating position.

You don't need to maintain a stiff posture. The goal is to stay relaxed while allowing your arms, wrists, and fingers to move freely.

2. Know the Piano and Keys

You don't need to learn music theory right away. As a beginner, you can start by learning numbered musical notation and familiarizing yourself with the position of each key on the piano. This is enough to get started with playing many popular songs.

However, if you want to move on to classical music, you'll need to learn basic music theory, including keyboard note names, the treble and bass clefs, notes and rests, key signatures, and time signatures. These fundamentals will help you read and understand traditional sheet music.

Tip 5. The 12 Repeating Notes and the 2 plus 3 Black Key Pattern

A piano keyboard contains 12 notes that repeat across different octaves. The notes are:

C, C sharp, D, D sharp, E, F, F sharp, G, G sharp, A, A sharp, B

After B, the sequence starts again at C in the next octave. You don't need to memorize all 88 keys individually. Instead, learn to recognize the repeating pattern of black keys.

Look closely at your keyboard:

  • Black keys are arranged in groups of two and three.
  • The white key immediately to the left of a group of two black keys is C.
  • The white key between the two black keys is D.
  • The white key immediately to the right is E.
  • The group of three black keys helps you locate F, G, A, and B.

Once you recognize this pattern, finding piano notes becomes much easier. Practice exercise: Find every C key on your keyboard, moving from the lowest octave to the highest. Then do the same for D, E, F, and the remaining notes. Repeat this exercise until you can identify the notes without counting the keys.

Tip 6. Find Middle C and Memorize Finger Numbers (1 to 5)

One of the most important notes for piano beginners is Middle C. Middle C is located near the center of an 88 key keyboard. It is the C closest to the middle of the piano and is commonly used as a starting reference for beginner exercises.

To find it:

  1. Locate a group of two black keys near the center of your keyboard.
  2. Find the white key immediately to their left.
  3. That white key is Middle C.

Next, learn the standard piano finger numbering system. Each hand uses five finger numbers:

  • 1 = Thumb
  • 2 = Index finger
  • 3 = Middle finger
  • 4 = Ring finger
  • 5 = Little finger

The numbering is the same for both hands. For example, when a beginner exercise tells you to play 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 with your right hand, you should use your thumb, index, middle, ring, and little finger in that order.

Practice identifying Middle C and placing both hands in a comfortable starting position. This simple exercise will prepare you for scales and beginner songs.

3. Play Beginner Level Songs

One of the best ways to stay motivated is to learn simple songs early in your piano journey. Even a short melody can make you feel that you're making real progress.

Tip 7. The 5 Finger Scale, from C to G and Back

The C major five finger position is one of the easiest ways to start developing finger coordination.

Place your right hand on the keyboard:

  • Thumb (1) on C
  • Index finger (2) on D
  • Middle finger (3) on E
  • Ring finger (4) on F
  • Little finger (5) on G

Now play the notes in order: C, D, E, F, G, F, E, D, C

Start with your right hand, then repeat the exercise with your left hand. Practice slowly and make sure each note sounds clear and even. Once you're comfortable, try playing the sequence with both hands together. Don't worry if your hands don't coordinate immediately. This skill develops gradually through repetition.

Tip 8. Learn How to Read Sheet Music

Learning to read piano sheet music might seem difficult at first, but it is an important skill if you want to play a wider range of songs.

Piano music typically uses two staffs together, known as the grand staff:

  • Treble clef: Usually used for higher notes, commonly played by the right hand.
  • Bass clef: Usually used for lower notes, commonly played by the left hand.

Each staff has five lines and four spaces. Every line and space represents a different musical note.

A useful way to memorize the lines is through these two phrases:

Treble clef, bottom to top:

  • Lines: E, G, B, D, F (Every Good Boy Does Fine)
  • Spaces: F, A, C, E (FACE)

Bass clef, bottom to top:

  • Lines: G, B, D, F, A (Good Boys Do Fine Always)
  • Spaces: A, C, E, G

Start by learning a few notes at a time rather than trying to memorize the entire staff immediately.

You can use free exercises on musictheory.net to practice identifying notes and understanding basic music notation. As you improve, you'll gradually recognize notes faster without having to count each line or space.

Tip 9. Interactive Apps, Pianote, flowkey and Simply Piano

Instead of following a traditional textbook alone, these apps combine video lessons, guided exercises, and song tutorials to help beginners understand what to play. Here are 3 popular options:

Pianote

Best for: Self taught beginners who want structured, concept first piano lessons.

Unlike listening apps that only grade correct keys, Pianote teaches through structured video lessons. You learn a technique, then apply it yourself. Each lesson includes a daily practice list, giving total beginners a real routine. The catalog also covers drums, guitar, and singing. A learner who tried every major app chose Pianote and still recommends it years later.

flowkey

Best for: Beginners who want to start playing songs fast with instant note feedback.

flowkey is a listening app. It hears your playing and grades whether you hit the correct keys. That real time feedback builds early momentum, but the experience focuses on which key to press rather than why. Concepts and technique take a back seat, so it works best as a practice companion alongside a structured course or teacher, not as a complete curriculum.

Simply Piano

Best for: Casual beginners who want a game like, low friction way to start.

Simply Piano sits in the same listening category as flowkey, grading you on correct keys. The interactive, game style feedback makes it approachable and keeps motivation high, but it teaches which key to push rather than the underlying concepts. Great as an on ramp or daily supplement, but pair it with a real course if you want lasting skill.

Other options worth exploring include Piano Marvel and Playground Sessions.

Which app should you choose?

You don't need to subscribe to every piano learning app. Pick one that matches your learning style, budget, and current skill level. Remember that apps are learning tools, not complete replacements for developing good technique and understanding music.

4. Choose How You'll Learn, Even on a Budget

You don't necessarily need expensive private lessons to start learning piano.

If you have a keyboard, a good learning resource, and enough discipline to practice regularly, you can make meaningful progress on your own.

The key is choosing a learning method that gives you enough structure to avoid jumping randomly between lessons and tutorials.

Tip 10. Free Videos and Online Courses

If you want to learn piano for free, there are plenty of online resources available.

YouTube is particularly useful because you can watch demonstrations, listen to examples, and repeat lessons whenever you need to.

Here are some resources worth checking out:

  • musictheory.net: Free music theory lessons and exercises covering notes, intervals, scales, and other fundamentals. Great for daily practice.
  • Alfred's Adult Piano Course: A structured method for adult beginners who want to learn piano step by step.
  • Bill Hilton: YouTube tutorials covering piano techniques, chords, and practical playing skills.
  • Faber Piano Adventures: A popular piano method with structured lessons and exercises for beginners.
  • Steve Lungrin: Beginner friendly piano lessons and demonstrations on YouTube.
  • Shawn Cheek: Piano tutorials and instructional videos for learners at different levels.
  • Marti Kluth: Piano teaching resources, including beginner focused lesson content.

You don't have to follow every teacher or course. Choose one main learning resource and stick with it for a while. Constantly switching between different teaching methods can make it harder to build a consistent learning path.

Tip 11. Follow a Structured Piano Book Learning Path

If you prefer learning from books, a structured piano method can help you understand what to practice and when to move on. Here is one possible progression for beginners:

Stage 1. Start with a beginner method book

Choose a beginner friendly series such as Faber Piano Adventures or Alfred's Basic Adult Piano Course.

Focus on:

  • Identifying notes on the keyboard.
  • Basic rhythm and note values.
  • Simple melodies.
  • Proper finger positioning.
  • Playing with one hand before introducing both hands.

Don't rush through the lessons. Make sure you can play each exercise comfortably before moving forward.

Stage 2. Introduce Beyer Piano Method (Op. 101)

Once you understand the basics, you can explore Beyer's piano exercises. Start with easier exercises and gradually work toward more challenging pieces. Exercises around the middle of the book can help you develop better coordination and reading skills.

Stage 3. Add Hanon Finger Exercises

When you have established a basic playing technique, you can introduce selected exercises from Hanon. These exercises focus on finger coordination, control, and evenness. Start slowly and avoid forcing your fingers to move faster than they comfortably can.

Stage 4. Introduce Czerny Op. 599

After developing a stronger foundation, Czerny's Op. 599 provides additional technical exercises to help improve finger independence and playing fluency.

Stage 5. Continue Developing Technique and Repertoire

You can gradually add more challenging Hanon exercises and explore additional pieces that match your skill level.

A useful reminder: You don't need to complete every classical exercise book to become a good recreational pianist. If your goal is to play pop songs, accompany yourself, or enjoy piano as a hobby, you can combine basic technical exercises with popular songs and chord based playing.

Choose music you actually enjoy. Learning becomes much more sustainable when you look forward to sitting down at the piano.

5. Do Basic Finger Exercises Every Day

Learning piano is not just about knowing which keys to press. Your fingers, hands, and brain need time to develop coordination and muscle memory. Regular practice is much more effective than practicing for several hours once in a while.

Tip 12. 10 to 30 Minutes a Day Beats 3 Hours Once a Week

Consistency is one of the biggest challenges for beginners. You might feel motivated to practice for three hours on your first day, but maintaining that schedule every week is difficult.

A better approach is to practice for around 30 to 45 minutes on most days, adjusting the duration to your schedule and energy level. Here is a simple daily piano practice routine:

1. Finger warm up (5 to 10 minutes)

Start with simple finger exercises or selected Hanon exercises.

  • Play slowly and evenly.
  • Start with a metronome at around 60 BPM.
  • Focus on relaxed wrists and controlled finger movements.
  • Increase the tempo gradually only when you can play accurately.

2. Technique and method books (20 to 30 minutes)

Work through your current beginner method book, such as Alfred, Faber, or Beyer. Instead of repeatedly playing an entire exercise from beginning to end, identify the sections that give you trouble. If you keep making mistakes in one measure, practice that section separately. You can also practice each hand independently before combining them.

3. Songs you enjoy (10 to 15 minutes)

Finish with a song you like. Good beginner choices include simplified versions of:

  • Ode to Joy
  • Happy Birthday
  • Canon in D
  • A simple version of A Thousand Years

The goal is to apply what you've learned while keeping the experience enjoyable. If you only have 10 to 15 minutes available on a busy day, that's still worthwhile. A short practice session is better than skipping the entire day.

Tip 13. Practice Slow and Use a Metronome

One common beginner mistake is trying to play too quickly. You might watch a piano tutorial and feel tempted to match the original speed immediately. However, playing quickly before you understand the notes and rhythm often leads to mistakes. Instead, practice slowly.

A metronome is a useful tool that produces regular beats to help you maintain a steady tempo. Here's how to use one:

  1. Choose a slow tempo, such as 60 BPM.
  2. Play one note or a short musical phrase with each beat.
  3. Make sure every note is clear and correctly timed.
  4. Repeat the phrase until you can play it comfortably.
  5. Gradually increase the tempo when you can play accurately.

You can find free online metronomes or use the built in metronome on many digital pianos. Remember: Accuracy comes before speed. Playing slowly with correct notes, rhythm, and relaxed hands is much more valuable than playing quickly with constant mistakes.

6. Avoid the Most Common Beginner Mistakes

It's easy to become impatient when learning piano. You might want to skip the basics and jump straight into your favorite songs. However, some shortcuts can make learning more difficult in the long run.

Here are two mistakes you should avoid.

Tip 14. Don't Skip the Fundamentals and Music Theory

Some beginners want to learn songs immediately without studying notes, rhythm, or basic music theory.

There is nothing wrong with starting with simple melodies or learning a song by ear. However, relying entirely on memorized finger movements can limit your progress.

For example, numbered notation (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7) can help you recognize basic melodies quickly. But if you only learn songs through numbers or memorized key positions, you may struggle when you encounter traditional sheet music or more complicated pieces.

Start building these fundamental skills:

  • Recognize the notes on the keyboard.
  • Understand basic note values and rests.
  • Learn how to read treble and bass clefs.
  • Understand simple time signatures.
  • Recognize basic major and minor scales.
  • Learn common chords and their relationships.

You don't need to master all of music theory at once. Spend a little time learning the fundamentals alongside your practical piano exercises. Over time, you'll find it much easier to understand new songs and read sheet music independently.

Tip 15. Don't Rush

One of the biggest reasons beginners become frustrated is trying to play songs that are far beyond their current skill level. You might dream of playing challenging pieces such as Mariage d'Amour (Chopin's Spring Waltz) or Croatian Rhapsody.

These are exciting goals, but jumping into advanced pieces too early can lead to frustration.

Instead, follow a gradual progression:

  1. Start with simple five finger exercises.
  2. Learn basic melodies using one hand.
  3. Introduce simple left hand accompaniment.
  4. Practice easy songs with both hands.
  5. Gradually move toward more complicated rhythms, chords, and pieces.

Don't compare your progress with experienced pianists on YouTube or social media. Everyone starts somewhere. Focus on improving one small skill at a time, and celebrate the songs you can play today.

FAQ

It depends on how often you practice, your learning method, and the difficulty of the song. If you practice regularly, you may be able to play a very simple melody within your first few days or weeks. A simplified version of Ode to Joy, for example, is a reasonable early goal.

However, playing a complete song smoothly with both hands takes more time. Developing good technique and reading music also requires consistent practice. Don't focus too much on how quickly you can finish a song. Focus on building the skills that will help you play more songs in the future.

Not necessarily before playing your first song, but learning to read sheet music is highly recommended if you want to develop your piano skills. Some beginners start by learning songs through YouTube tutorials, numbered notation, or playing by ear. These methods can help you get started and make learning enjoyable.

However, traditional sheet music gives you a much more flexible way to learn new pieces without relying entirely on video demonstrations or memorized finger movements. If reading music feels difficult, start with just a few notes. You can use musictheory.net to practice identifying notes on the treble and bass clefs. Remember these simple mnemonics: treble clef lines are EGBDF (Every Good Boy Does Fine) and bass clef lines are GBDFA (Good Boys Do Fine Always). Practice for a few minutes every day, and note recognition will gradually become faster.

If you can afford one, get a teacher. It's the most reliable way to build proper technique, music theory, and ear training, and it prevents bad habits you'd later have to unlearn. You should also seek a teacher if you're aiming for serious study or a conservatory, where talent alone isn't enough.

But if money is tight or you just want to play a few songs for fun, you can start self taught with method books and YouTube, focusing on songs you enjoy, and add a teacher later.

Conclusion

Learning piano from scratch can feel overwhelming at first, but you don't need to understand everything before you begin.

Start with a suitable digital piano, learn the basic notes and finger positions, and choose a structured learning resource. Then build a consistent practice routine and gradually challenge yourself with new songs.

You may notice meaningful improvements after a few months of regular practice, but becoming an advanced pianist takes much longer. Developing strong technique and musical understanding can take five to ten years or more, depending on your goals and practice habits.

Don't let the learning curve discourage you. Start small, practice consistently, and enjoy the process. Your first song is just the beginning of your piano journey.
