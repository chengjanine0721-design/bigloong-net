Is the piano a string instrument or a percussion instrument? The short answer is that it is both, and the longer answer is fascinating. Here is exactly how a piano makes sound, which family it really belongs to, and why you will hear two different labels.

This is one of those questions with a surprisingly good story behind it. On the surface, you sit at a keyboard and press keys, so it looks like a keyboard instrument. But the moment you hear the sound, something is clearly vibrating. So is piano a string instrument, or something else entirely? Let us follow the sound from your fingertip to the note you hear.

Quick answer The piano is officially a string instrument: each key drives a felt hammer that strikes metal strings. Because those strings are hit rather than plucked or bowed, it is also classed as a percussion instrument. In orchestras and instrument families it belongs with the strings.

Is the Piano a String Instrument?

Yes. In the standard system used by orchestras and musicologists, the answer to is piano a string instrument is yes, because sound is produced by vibrating strings. Inside every acoustic piano there are around 230 strings stretched tight over a cast-iron frame. When you press a key, a felt-covered hammer flies up and strikes them, and their vibration is what you hear.

That is the same basic principle as a violin or a guitar: a string vibrates and creates a pitched note. The mechanism that starts the vibration is different, but the source of the sound is a string. This is why the piano sits in the string family in almost every formal classification.

How a Piano Actually Makes Sound

Press a key and a chain of events happens in a fraction of a second. The key acts as a lever, lifting a damper off the strings and throwing a small hammer against them. The hammer rebounds immediately, leaving the strings free to ring. When you release the key, the damper falls back and stops the sound.

Higher notes use short, thin strings and lower notes use long, thick, copper-wound strings. The large, curved shape of a grand piano exists mainly to fit those long bass strings. None of this would matter if the instrument were simply a keyboard: the shape follows the strings, which tells you where the real sound source lies.

Is Piano a Percussion or String Instrument?

Here is where the second label comes from. Because a hammer hits the strings, you will often hear the question framed as is piano a percussion or string instrument. A percussion instrument is one that makes sound by being struck, shaken or scraped, and by that definition the piano qualifies: a hammer strikes the strings.

So both labels are accurate, just from different angles. The debate is really about which feature you are describing. The sound source is a string, so it is a string instrument. The sound trigger is a strike, so it has percussion character. When people argue is piano a stringed instrument or not, they are usually using the two words as if only one can be right, when in fact the piano sits neatly across both categories.

Tip A useful way to remember it: a violin's strings are bowed, a guitar's are plucked, and a piano's are struck. All three are string instruments; only the piano is also percussion.

What Family of Instruments Does the Piano Belong To?

In the four-family orchestral system — strings, woodwinds, brass and percussion — the piano is usually placed with the strings, though some schemes list it under percussion and others give it a special "keyboard" grouping of its own. That is the honest answer to what family of instruments does the piano belong to: it is officially a string instrument, with a foot in the percussion camp.

You may also see it described as a keyboard instrument. That term describes how you play it rather than how it makes sound, which is why an organ (which uses air) and a piano (which uses strings) can both be called keyboard instruments despite being completely different inside.

What About Digital Pianos?

A digital piano has no strings at all. It stores recordings of a real piano and plays them back through speakers when you press a key. Strictly speaking, then, a digital piano is an electronic instrument that imitates a string instrument. Players still call it a piano because it looks, feels and sounds like one, and because it is designed to reproduce the same technique.

FAQ

Is the piano a percussion instrument?

Partly. A felt hammer strikes the strings, so the piano produces sound by percussion. That is why it is sometimes grouped with percussion instruments. But because the vibrating source is a string, most formal classifications still call it a string instrument overall.

Why is the piano in the string section of an orchestra?

Because its notes come from vibrating strings, the same as violins and cellos. In orchestral writing the piano is treated as a string-toned instrument and is often listed in the string section, even though it is played from a keyboard.

Is a piano a stringed instrument like a guitar?

It is a stringed instrument in the sense that strings create the sound, but it is not played like a guitar. Guitar strings are plucked with the fingers or a pick, while piano strings are struck by hammers controlled from a keyboard. Both belong to the string family.

The Bottom Line

So, is piano a string instrument? Yes — its sound comes from vibrating strings, and that is its official family. Is it also percussion? Yes again, because hammers strike those strings. The two labels describe different parts of the same mechanism, not competing answers. Next time someone asks, you can say it is a string instrument that is played by percussion, and enjoy the puzzled look.
